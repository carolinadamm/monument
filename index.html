1891Jagodja, on the slopes of Kilimanjaro

Jagodja

A woman of Mamba, sister of the mangi Manamia

Kilimanjaro, 1891

My name is Jagodja. I was born on the slopes of the great mountain.

My brother is Manamia, mangi of Mamba. Mangi means chief. My family is of rank. My people are the Chagga.

“Chagga” – that is a word for outsiders. On the mountain we say: I am a Mamba.1 The people of Pare are our neighbours, and sometimes our enemies. The people of Meru and Masai too.

My grandmother says that before the strangers came, nobody here called himself black. Why would you? Everybody was. Now we hear it every day. Black. Native. Savage.

The strangers came and gave us a colour.

The first ones only looked. Traders from the coast came for ivory. Then white men who climbed up to the snow, and drew maps, and wrote in little books.

This year a new one came. His name is Peters.

He calls himself Reichskommissar. He says the mountain now belongs to something called German East Africa.

The first thing Peters built, before his own house was finished, was a gallows. So that everybody understands who rules. Then he took women.2 Daughters of chiefs, girls from the villages.

He took me.

The porters who carry things down to the coast say Peters is furious. They have a name for him already: mkono wa damu. The man with blood on his hands. They say he carries boxes, many of them, that contain our country’s soul.3

They also say that those white powerful men often take women for themselves. That makes me wonder about the country he came from. If there are more men, white, like him, how many more like me, forced concubines, must there be?

Now, this is what they call “German East Africa”.4

It is clear to everyone that I am as little German as I am “East African”. Nobody here has ever been East African.

I am a woman of Mamba.

Rosa Luxemburg

Socialist, member of the SPD

Berlin, 1914

Inspect this caricature closely. You have just met Jagodja. It shows how her story ends.56

Carl Peters had her hanged – you can see him in the middle with the gallows. My party leader accused him loudly in the parliament:7

“If your colonial policy has such consequences, then you have every reason to turn your backs on the whole of Africa as quickly as possible and to complete your work of civilization and culture here in Germany.”8

Nowadays it’s harder to excite the public for owning “exotic” territories. Every year, new scandals come to light, ranging from Peters’ unjust hanging of Jagodja to genocide.9 My party was glad to see Peters losing his post.

But this year? This year the Kaiser gave him his title back and a pension from his own pocket.10

Sorry. Did I forget to introduce myself?

My name is Rosa Luxemburg. I am 33 years old and have spent much of my political life fighting for socialism, democracy, and international solidarity among workers. Since the 1890s, I have been active in the Social Democratic Party of Germany (SPD).

But you wanted to know more about that monument. The German Colonial War Memorial was planned to be placed on Baltenplatz, the one you know as “Bersarinplatz” today.

A competition has been organised to build a grand German Colonial War Memorial in Berlin. It is supported by colonial circles, Reichstag politicians, and state authorities. It should be a reminder of their war successes abroad. Many supporters hope it will become a national shrine that strengthens enthusiasm for Germany’s colonial empire.11

The city suffers from what people already call a Denkmalseuche, a monument epidemic.12

Since German unification under Otto von Bismarck in 1871, the ruling classes have worked tirelessly to create a common national identity.13 The empire brought Prussians, Bavarians, Saxons, and many others under one flag. And monuments at every corner are supposed to remind the German people that they belong together.

But their boundary drawing didn’t stop there.

Feeling behind in the colonial race, many imperialists proclaim that “am deutschen Wesen soll die Welt genesen” (“the world shall be healed by the German spirit”).14 Now Germany is not only unified, but unified by the belief that its superior culture is destined to guide others.

From this belief grows militarism. Wars become proof of national strength. Military sacrifice becomes sacred. The planned monument is intended not merely to remember the (White) dead but to cultivate a national-colonial consciousness and strengthen Germany’s self-image as a colonial great power.15

And now, in 1914, this intoxication with power is driving Europe toward catastrophe.

I was born in Zamość, in the part of Poland ruled by the Russian Tsar. I am Jewish. I came to Germany in 1898 – and to become German, I married a man on paper.16 One signature, and I was a citizen. That is how solid a nation is: a stamp in a passport office.

For me, the nation is a cage built so that the common people have a reason to shoot each other.

“I feel at home in the entire world, wherever there are clouds and birds and human tears.”17

1914June, Pariser Platz, Berlin

Hedwig Heyl

Leader of the Women’s League of the German Colonial Society

Berlin, June 1914

My name is Hedwig Heyl. It’s still 1914, June to be precise, and I am sixty-four years old.

Since 1910 I have led the Women’s League of the German Colonial Society.18 The German Colonial Society that I lead has nineteen thousand members. We choose decent, hard-working German girls and send them to South West Africa as housekeepers and teachers – and, God willing, as wives.19

Too many of our men out there lived with native women. Their children were neither one thing nor the other. The government has now forbidden such marriages and taken German citizenship away from those children. Quite right.

A nation must know where its border runs – and the border runs through the family.

I have just walked through the exhibition here at the Academy on Pariser Platz, where the designs for our colonial warriors’ monument are shown.

That’s right, we’re building a monument!

I would like it to be built right here, in the centre. Can you believe they allocated a remote location like Baltenplatz?20 The money is there: 320,000 marks, granted by the Reichstag and the Bundesrat. A competition of the finest artists of the German Kaiserreich will determine the design – this is the exhibition I just looked at.

My favourite is Hahn and Sattler: a column twenty-five metres high, and on top a Victoria storming forward with sword and shield.21 Like our Victory Column.

Clear. Proud. Every child understands it.

Adolf Brütt designed to place a white rider on horseback, and the natives walk peacefully beside him. Exactly how it should be: we lead, they follow, and everyone is content.

And the winner? Fritz Behn, from Munich. An elephant. An African elephant on a pedestal!

It is exotic, I grant you. Strong. But where is the German in it? Where are our men, our sacrifice? We are honouring the soldiers who fell defending Germans overseas, and we put up a zoo animal.

I am not alone. The critics have torn it apart, and His Majesty has now ordered a second round of the competition.22 Let us hope sense returns.

I say the nation is a family. It has a father – the Kaiser. It has mothers – us. It has children who must be taught obedience, cleanliness, duty.

And like every family, it decides who sits at its table.

1932Bremen, at the unveiling of the elephant

Agnes von Boemcken

Chairwoman of the Women’s League of the German Colonial Society

Bremen, 1932

My name is Agnes von Boemcken.

This year, 1932, I became chairwoman of the Women’s League of the German Colonial Society – the league Hedwig Heyl led.23 Frau Heyl is eighty-two now and lives quietly in Berlin. I wish she had been here today. This, Bremen, is her hometown.24

Because look at what was just unveiled: ten metres of dark red brick. An elephant.

Yes – Fritz Behn’s elephant. The monument Berlin failed to build in 1914 was opened today.25

Why did it take so long? Why in a different city?

In short, the world war broke out. Rosa Luxemburg’s party, the Social Democrats, that’s right, they voted for war in 1914.

Meanwhile, the Allies stripped us of our overseas colonies and demanded impossible reparations. Decent German families were forced into poverty while profiteers and foreigners lived luxuriously in the cities.

Again in 1926, the colonial working group applied for the monument to be built in Bremen, because the association was represented strongly here.26

Half a decade later, the colonial question was revived. The Germany of today is not the Germany that emerged from defeat and humiliation.

Under Hitler, a new national consciousness is awakening.

The needs of nations remain. The need for space, opportunity, resources, and development remains.

On skulls and bones, shipped in from the South, the intellectual foundation for a different world order has been built.27 We are told by racial science that human groups possess distinct inherited characteristics and historical capacities. Many Germans therefore regard the preservation and cultivation of their own racial and cultural community as not merely a right but a duty.

This elephant symbolises our ongoing superiority. The German Reich will expand beyond its borders again.

2026Moshi, below the mountain, Tanzania

Jagodja

Eighteen, named after her great-great-great-aunt

Moshi, 2026

My name is Jagodja. I am eighteen.

I live near Moshi, below the mountain, in Tanzania. I am named after my great-great-great-aunt.

It’s the year 2026.

You heard Agnes von Boemcken talk about the skulls. Thousands of them are still in Berlin, in basements of prestigious research institutions and even family property.28 In 2023, scientists used DNA to match skulls with families from my region: an adviser of Mangi Meli, and Mangi Molelia of Kibosho and his relatives.

In February this year, two of Molelia’s descendants flew to Berlin to stand in front of the bones of their ancestors.29 The museum foundation has offered to give them back. Our own government has not answered yet.

But these are drops in the ocean.

Berlin has also changed some street names. Petersallee is gone since 2024; one part is now Maji-Maji-Allee. The Mohrenstraße was renamed Anton-Wilhelm-Amo-Straße in August 2025 – after more than thirty years of protest and a court fight until the day before.30

Thirty years for a street sign. That tells you how hard it is.

And the elephant? Later, during repairs, they took the words “Unseren Kolonien” (to our colonies) off it. In 1989 Bremen decided to turn it around, and in 1990, when Namibia became independent, they renamed it the Anti-Colonial Monument.31

Namibia’s president came and unveiled a plaque for the victims of German rule.32 In 2009 people brought stones from the Omaheke desert, where Herero families died of thirst after the battle at the Waterberg, and laid them in a circle beside the elephant.

I cannot speak for the Ovaherero and Nama; Namibia is almost as far from my home as Germany is. Many of them say Germany still owes them a real apology and reparations, not “development aid”.

The talks are not finished.
